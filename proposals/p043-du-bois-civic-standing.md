# Civic rights and the demand for respect

The short version and commentary are draft interpretations, not quotations.

## Short version

Defending voting, civic equality, and education is part of resisting racial devaluation.

## Context and limits

[Du Bois](../people/du-bois.md) presents a reasoned challenge to Booker T. Washington’s accommodation. Political exclusion and public prejudice reinforce one another, so surrendering rights is no route to respect. The passage allows voting restrictions applied equally to Black and white people; it should not be rewritten as an unqualified defense of universal adult suffrage.

## Source excerpt

> They do not expect that the free right to vote, to enjoy civic rights, and to be educated, will come in a moment; they do not expect to see the bias and prejudices of years disappear at the blast of a trumpet; but they are absolutely certain that the way for a people to gain their reasonable rights is not by voluntarily throwing them away and insisting that they do not want them; that the way for a people to gain respect is not by continually belittling and ridiculing themselves; that, on the contrary, Negroes must insist continually, in season and out of season, that voting is necessary to modern manhood, that color discrimination is barbarism, and that black boys need education as well as white boys.

English text, Chapter III. Historical language retained.

## Source and attribution

[W. E. B. Du Bois](../people/du-bois.md); [The Souls of Black Folk](../sources/du-bois-souls.md).

Passage: Chapter III, discussion following the demands for the vote, civic equality, and education; paragraph beginning “They do not expect”.
